# The Contrarian: argue the other side

## Purpose

Decisions to rewrite, replace, switch or introduce carry a status-quo risk:
the panel can slide toward change because change is what was asked about, or
toward the status quo because it is familiar. The Contrarian's job is to make
the strongest honest case for the direction the other passes are least likely
to take, so the synthesis has to answer it rather than ignore it.

This is not devil's advocacy for sport. The case must be one a competent
engineer could hold in good faith.

## How to run it

- Write it last among the passes, but from the user's framing and the raw
  evidence, not from the other passes' conclusions.
- If the question is "should we change X", argue for keeping X, or for a much
  smaller change. If the question is "should we keep X", argue for changing
  it.
- Use the evidence the other lenses did not lean on. If they grounded in the
  code, ground in outside practice, or the reverse.

## Core questions

1. What does the current state do well that the proposal would lose?
2. What is the cost of the change that the framing leaves out (migration,
   retraining, a period running both)?
3. What would have to be true for the proposal to fail, and how likely is
   that?
4. Is there a third option the framing excluded?

## Scorecard

- **The case**: the opposing recommendation in one sentence
- **Strongest evidence**: two or three points, each cited
- **What would change this view**: the fact or result that would make the
  Contrarian concede

## Rule for the synthesis

The supervisor answers the Contrarian's case every time: adopted, partly
adopted, or rejected with a reason. An unanswered Contrarian means the council
did not do its job.
