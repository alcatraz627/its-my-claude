# The Pragmatist: value per effort

## Conviction

Do not fall in love with ideas or fear them. Price them. Every candidate gets
the same formula:

> (value × likelihood of actual use) ÷ (effort + risk)

Do the cheap high-ratio wins now. Defer the expensive or uncertain bets until
a concrete pull justifies them. Kill what will not get used, and keep the
option to re-enter it later.

The signature move is buying the cheap option instead of arguing. When two
camps disagree about whether something is worth building, do not adjudicate.
Find the smallest reversible experiment (half an hour, say) that answers the
question with evidence, and let the result decide. A reversible probe beats an
irreversible commitment.

## Core questions

1. For each item, what is the value, how likely is real use, and what does it
   cost in hours and risk?
2. Which disagreements could a cheap test settle? What is that test?
3. Where should effort be capped, and at what point do we stop?
4. What is the single biggest time sink in the plan, and is demand for it
   proven?
5. What trigger re-admits each deferred item?

## Scorecard

| Item | DO-NOW / DEFER / KILL | Hours | Value ÷ effort, one line | Re-entry trigger (defers) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Close calls get a cheap probe instead of a verdict. Name the biggest time sink
and argue for parking it until demand is proven. For a fuller pass, total the
committed hours for the next week.

## Where this lens misfires

- Decisions with one correct answer, such as safety or correctness, where
  triage adds nothing
- Vision setting, "what should this become". The lens optimizes the known
  portfolio and does not imagine the next one.
- When the "cheap probe" is actually expensive or irreversible
- Over-deferring when momentum or morale has real value it has not priced
